# A stepper-driven rock tumbler

Build a rotary rock tumbler out of a NEMA 17, a roll of filament, and the
drawer of parts you already own. Then do the thing no commercial tumbler does:
make it *count*.

---

## The short version

A rotary tumbler is a barrel resting in the vee between two parallel rollers.
One roller is driven; friction carries the barrel round. Rocks, grit, water and
media slosh inside for weeks. That's it — the whole machine is a speed, a
cradle, and the patience to leave it alone.

Four design decisions do almost all the work:

| Decision | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Barrel speed** | ~45% of critical speed | Below it the load slides as a lump; above it, it centrifuges. One equation gives you the number. |
| **Roller diameter** | 40 mm | The roller *is* your gearbox. 40 mm puts a NEMA 17 at ~170 rpm, in its flat-torque region. |
| **Print material** | PETG or ASA — **never PLA** | A continuously-run stepper sits at 60–80 °C. PLA's glass transition is ~60 °C. |
| **Progress metric** | Barrel revolutions, not days | Abrasive wear is proportional to sliding distance. Days is a proxy; revolutions is the thing itself. |

**Recommended build sheet** (100 mm ID barrel, 40 mm rollers — generated by
`tools/tumbler_calc.py`):

```
  BARREL     inside dia  100.0 mm    critical speed  133.7 rpm
             target       60.2 rpm   (45% of critical)   86,661 rev/day
  CRADLE     roller dia   40.0 mm    contact angle    40.0 deg
             shaft spacing (c-c)     97.7 mm
             barrel ride height      58.2 mm above the shaft axes
  DRIVE      motor       168.5 rpm   torque at motor 11.05 N.cm
             (a NEMA 17 holds 40-50 N.cm — roughly 4x headroom)
             mechanical power         1.95 W
  CONTROL    8,987 usteps/s          TMC2209 VACTUAL = 12,565
```

Change any input and re-run the calculator; the OpenSCAD file reads the same
parameters and re-cuts the end plates to match.

## The one equation

A barrel stops tumbling when centripetal acceleration at the wall equals
gravity — the load pins itself to the wall and rides round in a useless ring.
Set ω²R = g and convert to rpm:

$$N_{critical} = \frac{42.3}{\sqrt{D}} \quad \text{(D in metres)}$$

That 42.3 is the mill constant from mineral processing. A rock tumbler is a
ball mill that has been told to relax.

- **Ball mills** run at 65–75% of critical. They want *cataracting* — media
  flying across the void and landing as impact, because they're trying to
  fracture rock.
- **Rock tumblers** run at roughly **35–50%** of critical. You want
  *cascading* — the load climbing one wall and sliding back over itself.
  Gentle abrasion, not fracture. Fracture is how you turn one nice agate into
  six disappointing chips.

A sanity check worth doing: the formula, at 45%, predicts **56 rpm** for a
4.5-inch barrel. A Lortone 3A — the machine that has sat on hobbyists' benches
since the 1960s — runs at 55–60 rpm. The physics and the folklore agree, which
is always a good sign.

Within the 35–50% band you have a genuine dial: run the coarse grind toward
50% for a brisk cascade, and drop the polish stage toward 35% so nearly-
finished stones aren't knocking lumps out of each other.

## Why a stepper is a strange choice, and how to make it a good one

Be clear-eyed: a stepper is not the obvious motor here. A £8 AC gearmotor spins
at 60 rpm, costs nothing, and doesn't care about anything. A stepper draws full
current whether loaded or not, runs hot, and needs a driver. For a machine that
runs 24/7 for six weeks, that's a real cost.

You have the steppers, so this is the right project for them — but the design
has to earn the choice back. It does, three times over:

**1. Silence.** A TMC2209 in StealthChop commutates with a quiet voltage-mode
PWM instead of an audible current chop. This is not a small thing. A tumbler
lives in your house for six weeks. The difference between a machine you leave
running and one you unplug on day three is entirely acoustic. A well-built
stepper tumbler is quieter than the rocks inside it.

**2. Exact, changeable speed.** Not "whatever the gearmotor came as" — 45.0% of
critical, held indefinitely, reprogrammable per stage with one register write.

**3. It can be closed-loop.** A magnet and a hall sensor let the controller
compare what it *commanded* with what actually *happened*. More on this below.

### The trick that makes it easy: VACTUAL

The TMC2209 contains its own step generator. Write a velocity to register
`0x22` and it spins by itself, indefinitely — **no STEP pulses from the
microcontroller at all**. No timer interrupt, no jitter, no CPU load. Speed
change and direction reversal are single register writes; the sign of the value
sets direction.

```
VACTUAL = usteps_per_sec × 2²⁴ / f_CLK        (f_CLK ≈ 12 MHz internal)
```

For a machine whose entire job is "hold one speed for six weeks," nothing else
comes close. Most people bit-bang step pulses for weeks and never learn this
register exists.

*Caveat worth printing on the lid:* the internal oscillator is only good to a
few percent. Treat the computed value as a starting point and trim it against
the measured revolution count — which the firmware logs anyway.

### The heat trap (read this one twice)

A stepper holding current continuously reaches 60–80 °C. PLA's glass transition
is around 60 °C. **A PLA motor mount will slowly sag over a six-week run**,
pulling the drive roller out of parallel, which causes the barrel to walk, which
causes it to climb off the rollers at 3 a.m. on day nineteen.

Print structural parts in **PETG or ASA**. And set the driver current *low* —
`IRUN = 8`, about a quarter scale. The torque demand is 11 N·cm against a NEMA
17's 40-plus. Every unnecessary amp is heat in a plastic bracket for a
thousand hours.

## The roller is your gearbox

This is the most useful lever in the build, and it's free — you just print a
different diameter. Friction drive preserves surface speed, so:

$$N_{roller} = N_{barrel} \times \frac{D_{barrel}}{d_{roller}}$$

A bigger roller means a slower motor and more torque demanded; a smaller roller
means a faster motor and less torque. You are choosing where to sit on the
stepper's torque curve, with a continuously variable ratio, using nothing but a
parameter change:

| Roller Ø | Motor rpm | Torque needed | Shaft spacing | µsteps/s |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 16 mm | 421 | 4.4 N·cm | 82.3 mm | 22,468 |
| 20 mm | 337 | 5.5 N·cm | 84.8 mm | 17,974 |
| 25 mm | 270 | 6.9 N·cm | 88.1 mm | 14,379 |
| **40 mm** | **169** | **11.1 N·cm** | **97.7 mm** | **8,987** |
| 50 mm | 135 | 13.8 N·cm | 104.1 mm | 7,190 |

40 mm is the sweet spot: the motor stays below 200 rpm where its torque curve
is still flat, the step rate is trivial for any driver, and there's ~4× torque
margin. Steppers make their best torque at low speed, and a friction drive is a
*speed increaser* — so bias toward the larger roller.

### Cradle geometry

With contact points at angle α either side of bottom dead centre:

```
shaft spacing = 2(R_barrel + r_roller)·sin α
ride height   =  (R_barrel + r_roller)·cos α
```

- **α < 30°** — cradle too shallow, the barrel wanders and walks off.
- **α ≈ 40°** — the sweet spot. Good traction, stable, easy to lift the barrel out.
- **α > 50°** — barrel sits deep, traction falls off, and it can climb.

`cad/tumbler.scad` computes this and cuts the bearing pockets accordingly.

## Don't print the barrel. Do print the liner.

**Don't print the barrel.** Three reasons, each sufficient: printed layer lines
are an invitation to leaks over a six-week wet run; silicon carbide grit
excavates layer boundaries; and a leaking tumbler barrel is a legendarily
miserable event involving abrasive slurry and every horizontal surface you own.

Good barrels, in order of preference:
1. **4" PVC DWV pipe** with a threaded cleanout adapter and plug — a real
   screw-top barrel for a few pounds, and genuinely watertight.
2. A **wide-mouth HDPE jar** with a gasketed screw lid.
3. An actual **Lortone/Thumler's rubber barrel**, if one turns up secondhand.

**Do print the liner.** This is the part that earns the printer its keep.
Commercial barrels are rubber-lined and hexagonal, for two reasons that both
matter:

- **The rubber** damps noise and cushions the stone.
- **The flats lift the load** so it cascades instead of sliding as a lump
  against a smooth wall. A bare PVC pipe tumbles badly. The same pipe with a
  hexagonal liner tumbles like a machine that cost ten times as much.

So: print a **hexagonal TPU sleeve** that press-fits inside the pipe
(`PART="hex_liner"`). TPU is exceptionally abrasion-resistant — it's what the
tyres are made of too, and it's most of why the machine is quiet.

## Revolutions, not days

Here's the part I'd actually be excited to build.

Abrasive wear follows **Archard's law**: the volume removed is proportional to
load × *sliding distance*, divided by hardness.

$$V = \frac{K \cdot W \cdot L}{H}$$

On this machine, sliding distance **is barrel revolutions**. Not hours —
revolutions.

Every tumbling instruction ever written says "run for seven days." That's a
proxy for the real dose, and a poor one: it silently assumes your barrel speed,
your charge, and your uptime match whoever wrote it. Count revolutions and you
measure the dose *directly*. Run the coarse stage to 600,000 revolutions and you
can change barrel size, change speed, lose a night to a power cut, and still
land in exactly the same place.

At 60 rpm that's 86,661 revolutions a day, so 600 krev ≈ 7 days — the
traditional number falls out as a special case. But now it's a *derived* number
rather than a rule of thumb, and it travels.

A magnet on the barrel end cap, a hall sensor on the frame, and a counter
persisted to flash. That's the whole mechanism, and it gives you:

- **Progress as a fraction of a real dose**, resumable across power cuts.
- **Slip detection.** Compare commanded rotation to measured rotation. Wet grit
  on a tyre shows up as slowly rising slip days before you'd notice by eye. A
  sudden jump to 100% means the barrel has stopped while the motor spins
  happily on — the failure that otherwise silently wastes a week of grinding.
- **A record.** After three batches you know what *your* machine does, in units
  that transfer to the next barrel you build.

### And while you're there: reverse it

Flip direction every six hours. The load stops packing into a preferred
channel, and tyre and barrel wear evenly. It costs one register write with a
sign flip. No commercial hobby tumbler does this, because a shaded-pole motor
can't.

## Build sequence

1. **Run the calculator** with your barrel's real measured OD.
   `python3 tools/tumbler_calc.py --barrel 100 --roller 40`
2. **Paste the numbers** into the parameter block of `cad/tumbler.scad` and
   render the parts:
   ```
   openscad -D 'PART="end_plate"'  -o end_plate.stl  cad/tumbler.scad
   openscad -D 'PART="roller_hub"' -o roller_hub.stl cad/tumbler.scad
   openscad -D 'PART="tyre"'       -o tyre.stl       cad/tumbler.scad
   openscad -D 'PART="motor_mount"' -o motor_mount.stl cad/tumbler.scad
   openscad -D 'PART="hex_liner"'  -o hex_liner.stl  cad/tumbler.scad
   ```
   Open it with no `-D` for a ghosted assembly view — worth a look to confirm
   the barrel really does sit where the arithmetic claims.
3. **Print**: 2 end plates, 2 roller hubs, 1 motor mount in PETG/ASA, 4
   perimeters, 30% infill. 2 tyres and 1 liner in TPU 95A.
4. **Assemble**: 608-2RS bearings press into the plates, 8 mm rod through,
   hubs grub-screwed to the rods, tyres stretched over the hubs. Bolt both
   plates to a plywood base.
5. **Drive**: GT2 belt from the motor to the driven roller, motor mounted
   *outboard* and *out of the drip zone*. Wet silicon carbide in a stepper's
   bearings ends the stepper.
6. **Wire** per the header comment in `firmware/main.py`, flash MicroPython,
   copy `tmc2209.py` and `main.py` to the Pico.
7. **Calibrate**: run it empty, count barrel revolutions against a stopwatch,
   trim `f_clk` until commanded and measured agree.

**Put the finished machine on concrete, a paver, or a sandbag** — anything but
a hollow wooden shelf, which is a loudspeaker with a tumbler for a driver.

## Actually tumbling things

Four stages, ~600 krev each. Same grit sequence everyone uses, because it works:

| Stage | Abrasive |
|---|---|
| 1. Coarse grind | 60/90 silicon carbide |
| 2. Medium grind | 120/220 silicon carbide |
| 3. Pre-polish | 500 SiC or 600 aluminium oxide |
| 4. Polish | cerium oxide or TXP aluminium oxide |

Rules that are not negotiable:

- **Barrel ⅔ to ¾ full** of rock, water just below the top of the load. An
  under-filled barrel doesn't cascade, it throws.
- **Add ceramic media or plastic pellets** as filler and cushioning, especially
  in the later stages.
- **One hardness per batch.** Mixing soft and hard stone on the Mohs scale gives
  you polished hard stones and destroyed soft ones.
- **Wash everything between stages.** Stones, barrel, lid, hands, the bench.
  One grain of 60 grit carried into the polish stage scratches every stone in
  the batch. The firmware deliberately does *not* auto-advance between stages,
  because it has no way of knowing whether you did this properly.
- **Never pour slurry down a drain.** It sets like concrete in the trap. Settle
  it in a bucket and bin the solids.

## Failure modes, ranked by how much time they'll cost you

| Failure | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Barrel walks off the rollers | rollers not parallel — often a sagged PLA mount | PETG/ASA; retaining flanges (already in the model) |
| Barrel slips, motor spins on | wet grit on the tyres | TPU tyres, drip tray, slip detection to catch it early |
| Stones chipped, not polished | speed too high — cataracting, not cascading | drop toward 35% of critical |
| Load slides as a lump | barrel wall too smooth | hexagonal liner |
| Leaks | printed or poorly gasketed barrel | screw-top PVC cleanout or HDPE jar |
| Grit-destroyed bearings | unshielded bearings in the splash zone | 608-**2RS** sealed, plus the drip tray |
| Motor cooked | driver current far higher than needed | `IRUN = 8`, and watch `DRV_STATUS` |

## Parallel lines of thought

A few directions this opens up, in rough order of how soon I'd chase them:

- **Direct barrel drive.** Skip the rollers: put the barrel on a shaft in its
  own bearing blocks and couple the stepper to it. Steppers are *happiest* at
  50–60 rpm, so this puts the motor exactly in its best region, and it
  eliminates slip entirely — the roller tumbler's main failure mode. You trade
  away easy barrel removal and a simple seal. Genuinely tempting for a v2.

- **DEM simulation before you cut anything.** The cascade is a granular flow
  problem, and discrete element method tooling (LIGGGHTS, or Blender's rigid
  body solver for something quick and visual) will show you the difference
  between 35% and 50% of critical, and between a round barrel and a hex one,
  without a week of waiting. The tumbling-mill DEM literature is large and
  directly transferable.

- **Acoustic monitoring.** The sound of the barrel *is* a measurement. Cascading
  and sliding have distinctly different spectra, and a cheap MEMS microphone
  plus an FFT on the Pico could close the loop on the *quality* of the tumbling
  action rather than just its speed. Tune the speed until the spectrum says
  "cascading." Ball-mill operators have done acoustic load monitoring for
  decades; nobody has bothered at hobby scale.

- **Instrumented dose–finish curves.** You now have a controlled dose variable.
  Stop a batch at 300, 450, 600, 750 krev and measure surface finish. You'd
  have an actual Archard curve for your local rock, which is a small piece of
  genuinely novel data — nobody in the hobby publishes this because nobody
  counts.

- **Vibratory conversion.** Vibratory tumblers finish in days rather than weeks
  and preserve shape rather than rounding. Different machine, same electronics,
  and your steppers with off-centre masses would do it.

## Sources and further reading

**The physics**
- Wills, B.A. & Finch, J.A., *Mineral Processing Technology*, 8th ed.,
  Butterworth-Heinemann, 2015 — ch. 7 on tumbling mills: critical speed,
  cascading vs cataracting, charge motion. This is the real literature behind
  the rules of thumb.
- Archard, J.F., "Contact and Rubbing of Flat Surfaces," *Journal of Applied
  Physics* 24(8), 1953 — the wear law that justifies counting revolutions.
- Rabinowicz, E., *Friction and Wear of Materials*, 2nd ed., Wiley, 1995 — for
  three-body abrasive wear, which is exactly what grit between rock and barrel is.

**The craft**
- Hobart King, [rocktumbler.com](https://rocktumbler.com) — the standard hobby
  reference. Grit sequences, barrel speeds, troubleshooting. Written by a
  geologist, unusually careful about what's evidence and what's tradition.
- The Lortone 3A and Thumler's Model B manuals — worth reading as design
  documents. Sixty years of refinement in very few parts.

**The electronics**
- Trinamic / Analog Devices, *TMC2209 Datasheet*, rev 1.09 — §5 (single-wire
  UART), §6 (register map). `VACTUAL` is §6.3; `IHOLD_IRUN` is §6.2. Dense but
  excellent.
- Mike Cook, *Raspberry Pi Cookbook*, and the MicroPython `machine` docs for
  the Pico side.

**Going further**
- Cleary, P.W., "Charge behaviour and power consumption in ball mills:
  sensitivity to mill operating conditions," *Int. J. Mineral Processing*
  63(2), 2001 — the DEM paper to start from if you chase the simulation idea.
